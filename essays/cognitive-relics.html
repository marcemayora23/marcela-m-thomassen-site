Cognitive Relics

August 2026

"The test of all beliefs is their practical effect in life." — Attributed to Helen Keller, Optimism (1903)

I believe there are at least three kinds of inheritance, though we tend to speak openly about only two.

The first is material: money, property, jewelry, and the possessions passed from one generation to the next, whether abundant, scarce, or absent altogether. The second is biological: the genes we inherit without ever having asked for them, from the color of our eyes to our predisposition to certain illnesses. Both are tangible, measurable, even litigable. There are lawyers for the former and laboratories for the latter.

The third kind of inheritance leaves no legal record and no biomarker. It cannot be argued over in court or detected in a blood test. Instead, it is quietly passed down from one mind to another.

This is cognitive inheritance: the mental frameworks that form the foundation of our worldview, passed to us through family, culture, history, and social institutions, often before we are even capable of questioning them.

When it comes to material inheritance, we choose to throw away, upgrade, or keep the things we like. Yet we rarely stop to do the same with the seemingly invisible mental models passed down to us, even though they shape nearly every experience we will ever have.

If you dare to put yourself through this journey, you will come across what I've come to recognize as cognitive relics: psychological adaptations that originated in contexts that no longer exist, yet continue to organize our perception and drive our behavior as though they do.

The idea is not entirely new. In The Selfish Gene, evolutionary biologist Richard Dawkins proposed the concept of the meme: a unit of cultural transmission that spreads from mind to mind, replicating itself through human communication. Like genes, beliefs can persist across generations because they are good at replicating, not necessarily because they are universally beneficial or true.

This distinction matters. Truth matters, but so do the practical effects of believing something. A belief can be true and still have harmful consequences; a belief can also have once served a useful purpose and become maladaptive when its context changes. Rather than asking whether a belief is simply right or wrong, cognitive relics invite a different question:

What problem was this belief originally solving, and does it still solve that problem today? If not, does the belief still help us, or has it become a relic?

"The unexamined life is not worth living." — Socrates, as recorded by Plato in Apology

So I started examining mine.

I found that religion, specifically the fundamentalist form of Catholicism that I inherited, was a major cognitive relic within it. It provided a comprehensive worldview, a way of life, and explanations for some of life's biggest questions:

Who am I?
Why am I here?
How did the universe come to be?
What happens after death?
What is right and what is wrong?

The psychologist and philosopher Erich Fromm described something similar through his concept of a frame of orientation: the mental structure we use to understand ourselves, others, and the world around us. Humans, Fromm argued, need more than physical survival; we also need meaning, direction, and a coherent way to navigate existence. Religion can provide such a frame, but it is not the only possible frame.

I used to take the Catholic framework as the literal, absolute Truth, rather than seeing it for what I now believe it to be: one hypothesis among many, one way of living among many.

This realization was difficult to metabolize. I went through a period of deep psychological distress as I began uncovering just how deeply the Catholic frame of orientation had shaped my sense of self, morality, and relationship with authority.

How could I have believed that I was fortunate enough to be "born into the one true religion," while everyone else simply wasn't so "lucky"? Why me? Why God? And why "my" God?

The idea of religion as a cognitive relic deserves far more exploration than I can give it here. I have my own conclusions and may explore them in future essays. For now, I'm less interested in the metaphysical question of whether its claims are true and more interested in the practical effects that living according to these beliefs had on my human experience.

One of the realizations that broke my heart was that I had learned to outsource my personal responsibility to an external "divine" authority rather than learning to trust myself, listen to my body, and develop my own judgment.

I've lived most of my life with the practical consequences of that belief. Since birth, the underlying message reinforced by my environment, and one that came to govern my inner world, an inner script that was essentially:

Who are you to presume you know better? You must defer to God, Tradition, and Authority. Otherwise it isn't valid. It isn't real.

God was the framework through which I self-identified and filtered experience. I constantly asked myself: What does God want me to do? What does he expect from me? When something went wrong, I would think: It's God's will, even though I might not understand it.

At its core, the cognitive relic was not simply a wholesale explanation of existence. It was the belief that I wasn't the legitimate authority on my own experience, a belief that put a cap on how much I was allowed to trust my own mind.

And this had another consequence: it put a ceiling on my curiosity.

My hypercuriosity, which I consider fundamental to who I am, was suppressed. If I had been handed the answers to questions that are at the core of human existence, why would I seek further explanations? Why would I need to inquire, or even ponder them?

When I started reading again at 22, this part of me woke up. What began as seemingly identity-threatening questions slowly transformed into a sense of safety in curiosity. That curiosity became my guide towards the conclusions I'm detailing here, nearly a decade later.

Over the years, I became more comfortable saying "I don't know." I began to see that the Catholic framework isn't the final word on existence, and neither is any other framework that claims to be, religious or not.

In fact, I now believe that we still don't have a definitive answer to the question of the origins of the universe. But not having a definitive answer doesn't mean that every explanation is equally plausible. We can still discard some options, and I think we have enough evidence to reject the Judeo-Christian concept of God.

But I also see now that examining our inherited beliefs is not simply about replacing one set of answers with another. It is about examining what those beliefs do to us, asking whether they expand our human experience or constrain it.

As Helen Keller wrote, "The test of all beliefs is their practical effect in life."

So I've chosen to reject God as a framework for living without claiming to have solved God as a metaphysical question. I don't need to know what ultimately exists beyond our understanding in order to decide how I want to live my life.

I've never felt more free, and I've never felt more responsible for how I live and how my actions affect others.

It feels heavier, but in a good way.

This is what makes cognitive inheritance so consequential. We cannot choose our genes, nor can we retroactively decide the wealth or country into which we were born. But we can, however painfully, examine the assumptions we inherited and consciously decide whether they still deserve a place in our lives.

Those questions matter not only for ourselves, but for the generations that follow. Every belief we leave unexamined becomes part of another person's cognitive inheritance. If we choose to have children, we also choose, consciously or not, which cognitive relics we will pass on.

Dear reader,
I dare you to examine your own cognitive inheritance.

What did you inherit before you were capable of questioning it? What problems were those beliefs originally solving? And do they still deserve a place in your life?

I believe it is our moral responsibility to ask these questions. Perhaps this is one way we can make the world a better place: not by handing the next generation better answers—or answers at all—but by giving them the freedom to think for themselves and ask better questions.

Marcela M. Thomassen
